# Game Quality Principles

**Purpose:** general principles for judging a delivered game. They say what good means, not how a particular game must look or play. The criteria writer turns them into task-specific, checkable claims; this document sets no sizes, counts or other fixed numbers, and never prescribes a genre, a style or a plot.

## What good means

1. **It works as asked.** The game starts, can be played, ends with a result and can be played again; everything the Task asks for is there and works. The rules the player sees are the rules the game follows.
2. **The player understands it by playing.** Goals, controls and threats can be grasped from the screen, without reading the code or a manual.
3. **Control is immediate and fair.** Every input gets a visible response at once; what happens follows from the player's choices; danger can be read before it strikes; a loss can be explained from what was on screen.
4. **The experience has a shape.** It opens, builds and arrives somewhere: challenge grows in steps the player can follow, and there is a goal, a peak or an ending worth reaching.
5. **Choices matter.** The player decides something that changes the outcome, and there are reasons to play again.
6. **Look and feel serve play.** One coherent style, true to what the Task asks for, that helps the player see what matters. Looks are judged against published games of the same genre and style, never against fixed rules for size, colour or space.
7. **It is finished.** Every screen the flow needs is there; nothing reads as debug output or a placeholder; it keeps working through normal use.

## What taste means

Working is the floor. Taste is what makes a player remember the game, and it is where the proactive challenges live.

1. **Feel.** Every action lands: a hit has weight, a move has anticipation and follow-through, an important moment
   stops, shakes or slows the world for an instant. Good feel is not noticed; its absence makes the game feel empty.
2. **One world.** Picture, sound, interface, text and rules tell the same story. The menus, fonts and numbers belong
   to the game's world, not to a web form laid over it.
3. **Restraint.** Effects serve what matters now. The brightest, loudest, fastest thing on screen is the most
   important thing at that moment; taste shows as much in what is left out as in what is added.
4. **Rhythm.** Tension and release alternate; a peak is prepared and followed by a breath; the first moments make the
   player want to go on.
5. **Care in the details.** Small things reward a closer look: an idle gesture, traces of life in the world, a
   surprise found only by paying attention. The player feels that someone cared.
6. **A face of its own.** Nothing looks like a default or a template (default colours, fonts, interface); the game
   has a recognisable identity within the style the Task asks for.
7. **A feeling to leave with.** The game sets out to make the player feel something (thrill, warmth, dread, wonder)
   and does.

## Ambition

Proactive challenges ask for what the best games of this kind do and what would surprise a player: a signature
moment, a mechanic that changes how the game is played, a scene people would share. Polishing what is there is not
a challenge. Each challenge still names one property a player can see or do in one short session.

## How to judge (evidence)

- **Play to know.** Behaviour (scoring, collisions, winning, losing, restarting) is shown by running the game and observing the state, by natural play or a controlled setup through the game's own functions. Source code shows intent; a run shows behaviour.
- **Failed is not unverified.** When a run never reached the situation, the claim is unknown, not unmet. When it reached it and the behaviour was wrong, it is unmet.
- **Looks by eye.** Visual claims are judged from screenshots and close-ups, as a player would see them.
- **Taste by play and eye.** Feel, rhythm and atmosphere are judged from what a run shows (a sequence of screenshots
  through the moment, the state over time), against the best published games of the same kind; code that sets up an
  effect shows intent, not the result.
- **Concrete, not fixed.** Each claim names one observable property of this game, settleable in one short session. Taste is not turned into numbers.
- **Name where it fails:** the screen or moment, what was expected, how to see it again.

## Craft references (insight only)

Look for teaching through play, readable danger, fair outcomes, precise and forgiving control, motion that announces what comes, and a coherent artistic vision. Transfer the principle, never another game's genre, content or budget.
